Major project · 2026–27

Shadows into molecules

We take millions of extremely noisy 2D images of frozen molecules, each photographed at an unknown random angle, reconstruct the 3D structure — and then recover how the molecule moves.

Start here

Proteins are machines, and shape is everything

Proteins are the tiny machines inside every living thing. They digest your food, carry oxygen in your blood, copy your DNA, and fight infections.

What a protein does is decided entirely by its 3D shape. Change the shape and it stops working — that's what a lot of diseases physically are. Almost every drug works by sticking to a specific shape.

So: if you want the shape, you have to see it.

The obstacle

They are far too small to see

This isn't a "we need a better microscope" problem. A normal microscope uses light, and light waves are simply too big — roughly a hundred times wider than the thing you're trying to look at. It's like trying to feel the texture of a coin while wearing boxing gloves.

The workaround

Use electrons instead of light

Electrons behave like waves too, but far, far smaller ones — small enough to resolve a molecule. So you fire a beam of electrons through the sample and record what comes out the other side.

One catch: the electron beam destroys the molecule almost immediately. So first you freeze everything in ice, which holds the molecule still and helps it survive the beam a fraction longer.

Freezing plus electrons. That's cryo-EM, and it won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2017.

What you actually get

Three problems, all at once

  1. 01
    It's flat
    The beam passes straight through, so you get a shadow — a 2D projection of a 3D object. One whole dimension is gone.
  2. 02
    It's buried in noise
    Because electrons destroy the sample, you use as few as you possibly can. The result is so noisy that you genuinely cannot see the molecule by eye. It looks like TV static.
  3. 03
    You don't know the angle
    Millions of copies of the molecule are frozen in the ice, each tumbled to a random orientation. Nobody recorded which way any of them were facing. You have to work that out too.
The core idea

One image is useless. Millions are enough.

Any single shadow tells you almost nothing. But a shadow taken from a different angle tells you something different — and if you have millions of them from every direction, together they pin the 3D shape down completely.

The catch is that you must work out the shape and the camera angle of every single picture at the same time, using nothing but the pictures.

Reconstruct a statue from a million blurry shadows, when nobody wrote down where the camera was standing.
Part one

Get the shape

This is the classical problem. It's hard, but it's solvable — that's what the Nobel was for. We build it from scratch anyway, because we need to own every piece of the pipeline before we can change any of it.

  1. 01
    Fake the data
    Take a structure we already know, and generate realistic fake cryo-EM images from it. Now we have an answer key to check ourselves against.
  2. 02
    Rebuild it — angles known
    Cheat slightly: tell the algorithm what angle each image was taken from, and recover the 3D shape. The warm-up.
  3. 03
    Rebuild it — angles unknown
    Take the cheat away. Now solve for the shape and all the angles together, which is the real problem.
Part two

Molecules don't hold still

Here's the thing everyone glosses over. A protein isn't a statue. It hinges, twists, opens and closes. And that motion isn't a nuisance — the motion is the job. A protein that pumps something across a cell membrane does it by physically opening and shutting.

Which means every one of your million frozen images caught the molecule at a different moment of that movement. You're not photographing one shape. You're photographing a whole movie, one random frame at a time, out of order.

Recovering that movie is unsolved. That's our research.

  1. 04
    Recover the motion the research
    Not one shape — the full range of shapes the molecule moves through, and how common each one is.
Our contribution

Everyone is using the wrong ruler

To recover motion, software has to compare shapes and ask "how different are these two?" Every existing method answers that by laying the two shapes on top of each other and subtracting them point by point.

That sounds reasonable and it is quietly wrong. A molecule changing shape is material physically moving through space. Subtraction can't see movement — it only sees "stuff vanished here, stuff appeared there."

How we know if it worked

One molecule, two movies, side by side

We run the leading existing method and ours on the same data, and play both results. Theirs dissolves. Ours flows. If someone can see the difference in three seconds without us explaining anything, we've won.

There's also a scoreboard: CryoBench, a public benchmark where the true answer is known, so our accuracy is a number and not an opinion.

Honest risks

What could go wrong

  • Optimal transport is entering this field right now. A well-funded lab could publish our idea before we finish.
  • We have to actually beat the existing methods on the benchmark. We might not.
  • Rung 03 — solving for shape and angles together — is the time sink. If it runs long, everything after it gets squeezed.

Worth saying: even if rung 04 doesn't beat anyone, a working cryo-EM reconstruction engine built from nothing is already a real project.

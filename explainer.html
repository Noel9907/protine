Major project · 2026–27

Shadows into molecules

We take millions of extremely noisy 2D images of frozen molecules, each photographed at an unknown random angle, reconstruct the 3D structure — and then recover how the molecule moves.

Start here

Proteins are machines, and shape is everything

Proteins are the tiny machines inside every living thing. They digest your food, carry oxygen in your blood, copy your DNA, fight infections.

What a protein does is decided entirely by its 3D shape. Change the shape and it stops working — that is what many diseases physically are. Almost every drug works by sticking to one specific shape.

So if you want to understand it, you have to see it.

The obstacle

They are far too small to see

This is not a "buy a better microscope" problem. Normal microscopes use light, and light waves are simply too big — about a hundred times wider than the thing you're looking at. Like feeling the texture of a coin through boxing gloves.

The workaround

Use electrons instead of light

Electrons behave like waves too, but far smaller ones — small enough to resolve a molecule. So you fire a beam of electrons through the sample and record what comes out the other side.

One catch: the beam destroys the molecule almost immediately. So first you freeze everything in ice, which holds it still and helps it survive a fraction longer.

Freezing plus electrons. That is cryo-EM, and it won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2017.

What makes it hard

Three problems, all at once

  1. 01
    It's flat
    The beam passes straight through, so you get a shadow — a 2D projection of a 3D object. A whole dimension is gone.
  2. 02
    It's buried in noise
    Because electrons destroy the sample, you use as few as you possibly can. The result is so noisy you genuinely cannot see a molecule by eye, as the image above shows.
  3. 03
    You don't know the angle
    Every molecule froze in a random orientation and nobody recorded which. You have to solve for that too.
The core idea

One image is useless. Millions are enough.

A single shadow tells you almost nothing. But a shadow from a different direction tells you something different — and millions of them, from every direction, pin the 3D shape down completely.

The catch is you must solve for the shape and the camera angle of every single image at the same time, using nothing but the images.

Reconstruct a statue from a million blurry shadows, when nobody wrote down where the camera was standing.
Part one

Get the shape

The classical problem. Hard, but solvable — that is what the Nobel was for. We build it from scratch anyway, because we need to own every piece before we can change any of it.

  1. 01
    Fake the data
    Take a structure we already know and generate realistic fake cryo-EM images from it. Now we have an answer key.
  2. 02
    Rebuild it — angles known
    Cheat slightly: tell the algorithm each image's angle, recover the 3D shape. The warm-up.
  3. 03
    Rebuild it — angles unknown
    Take the cheat away. Solve for the shape and every angle together. The real problem.
Part two

Molecules don't hold still

Here is the part everyone glosses over. A protein is not a statue. It hinges, twists, opens and shuts. And that motion isn't a nuisance — the motion is the job.

Now the problem. Every one of your million frozen images caught the molecule at a different moment of that movement. You are not photographing one shape. You're photographing a whole movie, one random frame at a time, out of order, and the frames aren't labelled.

Recovering that movie is unsolved. That is our research.

  1. 04
    Recover the motion the research
    Not one shape — the full range of shapes the molecule moves through, and how common each one is.
Our contribution

Everyone is using the wrong ruler

To recover motion, the software constantly compares shapes and asks "how different are these two?" Every existing method answers by stacking the two shapes and subtracting them point by point.

That sounds reasonable and it is quietly wrong. A molecule changing shape is material physically moving through space. Subtraction cannot see movement — it only sees "stuff vanished here, stuff appeared there."

Why this matters now

AlphaFold solved the shape. It did not solve the motion.

You may have heard that AI already solved protein structure. Half true. AlphaFold predicts one shape per protein, brilliantly. It cannot tell you how that protein moves — proteins that genuinely flex come back as static, low-confidence mush.

There is a review paper titled, almost word for word, "AlphaFold and protein folding: not dead yet. The frontier is conformational ensembles." That frontier is rung 04.

~1 in 3Of all drugs target GPCRs — receptors that work by changing shape
~17,000Structures cryo-EM has contributed to the public protein database
~29,000Density maps already sitting in the public archive, free to download

The drug targets that matter most — receptors, ion channels, transporters — are machines. A transporter works by opening and closing. Miss the motion and you miss the mechanism.

The bottleneck is software, not microscopes

This is the part that makes four students with laptops relevant. The instruments already work. The images already exist, sitting in public archives. What's missing is the algorithm that pulls the motion out of them.

A better method retroactively improves data that was collected years ago.

That is real leverage, and it costs nothing but maths. Being honest about the ceiling though: we would not cure anything. We would make a tool, and other people would use it. Worth knowing that cryoSPARC — now in nearly every structural biology lab on earth — started life as academic software too.

How we know if it worked

One molecule, two movies, side by side

We run the leading existing method and ours on the same data and play both. Theirs dissolves. Ours flows. If someone sees the difference in three seconds without us explaining anything, we've won.

There's also a scoreboard: CryoBench, a public benchmark where the true answer is known, so our accuracy is a number and not an opinion.

Honest risks

What could go wrong

  • Optimal transport is entering this field right now. A well-funded lab could publish our idea before we finish.
  • We have to actually beat the existing methods on the benchmark. We might not.
  • Rung 03 — shape and angles together — is the time sink. If it runs long, everything after gets squeezed.

Worth saying plainly: even if rung 04 beats nobody, a working cryo-EM reconstruction engine built from nothing is already a real project.
